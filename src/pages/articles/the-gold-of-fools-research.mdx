# Why during a search I see treasure everywhere

During July 2026, I was in Agrigento, Sicily, Italy, looking for minerals. The thought occurred to me during my fossil hunting first in Maddalusa and then in Giazza. After spending some time looking through rocks, every streak, every fracture of irregular shape, every stone that slightly resembled an elongated form became an ammonite to me. Naturally, most of them turned out to be just plain old stones. But each one, for just a split second, before being rejected as useless, did get a chance.

It's not a discovery, just an observation which anyone who has been looking for mushrooms, minerals or even a parking spot can make: at a certain point, everything starts resembling what you are looking for. Frosty glass becomes quartz. A tile sparkles and for a moment it's gold. An ordinary stone looks as "it should." The psychological process at work here is connected with the lowering of the criteria of similarity by the mind, actively engaged in a search process.

## What I think is: It’s not that your eyesight gets better, rather you have fewer standards for acceptance.

The wrong intuition here is that seeking will make you better at identifying a target. And what I think happens is quite the reverse, or more precisely: a bit of both. The sensitivity of your visual system to distinguishing gold from pyrite does not go up. What goes down is how strict you are in accepting an object as a plausible candidate.

This very important concept, that of *sensitivity* vs. *criterion*, is one of the foundations of the Signal Detection Theory, a theory developed in the 1950s originally in radar and psychophysics, and which has been used ever since to model just about any situation when there is the need to discriminate the true signal from noise.

I did some SOTA work (I hate doing it), but there is an even more ancient example: In the 1940s, Bruner & Postman demonstrated that being in an active mental state biases the perceptual system towards making sense of ambiguous stimuli according to current expectations, what they called *perceptual set*. It's not just random pareidolia (like seeing faces in clouds, which we all do automatically all the time). It's pareidolia conditioned by task.

## My take on it

As I'm not a cognitive psychologist (though my girlfriend is), this is just a quick napkin drawing to structure my thoughts, not a fully developed and verified theory.

If the key issue is that the criterion lowers, then this is what should be in the equation, rather than some vague "perceived value." Denote c(t) as the similarity criterion above which an object x becomes a "plausible candidate" for inspection, where t is the time since the start of the session. Then:

$$c(t) = c₀ − I(t) · e^(−λ·d(x,T))$$

where:
- c₀ is the "resting" threshold, which I have without any search going on (the stone is a stone and I am not picking it up);
- d(x,T) is the distance between the stimulus x and the target object T which I am looking for (a true ammonite has a d = 0, a regular stone will have a big d);
- I(t) is the intensity of the search state right now, how "in hunting" my brain is; and
- λ controls how fast the effect drops off away from the target.

The closer the match between stimulus and what I am looking for (small d), the greater the decrease in the threshold, which is needed to clear it and for the stone to be grabbed and flipped over and examined. This distance-based decay effect is a concept taken from Shepard (1987), who was the first to prove that perceptual generalization follows exactly this kind of exponential curve.

Intuition tells me (I(t)) that it is not a constant value but that it increases while you search in vain (similar to a slot machine, where intermittent reinforcement ensures that your attention is kept high) and then drops quickly as soon as you stop searching, as you go home, as you switch off the "hunting" mode. For example:

$I(t) = α · (1 − e^(−β·t))$ during the search

which rises slowly, and after the search process is completed, the fall is very rapid. What goes up is not the rating of the stimulus, but rather the threshold of acceptance on my part; the ammonite doesn’t look any prettier because I’m looking for it, but rather I accept less and less like ones.

## Some predictions

Given the postulated mechanism of a systematic reduction of the criterion, induced by the search state, the equation leads to precise, predictable results:

**1. The greater the similarity of the stimulus to the target, the higher the probability that it will pass the threshold and be detected/inspected.** A sparkling glass item is better able to deceive a person than a plain stone. Such probability declines as one departs perceptually from the prototype of the object being sought.

**2. The stronger the need to search, the lower is the threshold of detection.** A person who searches in a hurry (time is short, high stakes) will accept progressively less similar to the target stimuli than a person who searches leisurely.

**3. The threshold decreases gradually over time and rises quickly after the search stops.** The more one searches without success, the more relaxed is the criterion (the principle of intermittent reinforcement that keeps gamblers on the slot machines). However, as soon as the search is stopped and one leaves the state of mind characteristic of the search, the criterion should rise quickly again.

And the third prediction is, by far, the most interesting, since neither one of the traditional explanations (pareidolia, category priming) gives this exact curve: the decrease of the threshold throughout the experiment and the almost instantaneous increase of the threshold right at the end of the experiment.

It is neither my discovery nor a novel one. Signal Detection Theory has been around for seventy years. Perceptual Set, according to Bruner and Postman, for eighty. I didn't discover anything that hasn't been said before, and the formulas I've provided are nothing but a personal exercise in understanding things clearly.

All I learned from Maddalusa and Giazza is that psychologists had long been aware that looking for something not only increases your attentiveness, but also predisposes you to believe in lies. This is a very well-known truth for any amateur paleontologist, mycologist, or minerologist, who does not need any equations to understand it.

Next time a piece of concrete briefly looks like a fossil to you, it isn't a flaw in your perception. It's just the small, harmless price of searching for something with enough eagerness to find it.
