# Structural boundaries, partitions and resonance of radical-prime multiplicative sequences

## 1. Introduction

In multiplicative number theory, the relationship between an integer $k$, its squarefree kernel (the radical), and its arithmetic divisor functions turns out to draw some fairly intricate boundaries within the natural numbers. This paper collects empirical computations and analytical proofs for sequences where an integer is generated by the product of its radical and a prime-factor-counting metric.

To establish a rigorous foundation, we define the following arithmetic functions and sequence classes for any positive integer $k > 1$ with canonical prime factorization $k = p_1^{a_1} p_2^{a_2} \cdots p_m^{a_m}$:

* **Little Omega Function ($\omega(k)$ — [A001221](https://oeis.org/A001221)):** The number of distinct prime factors of $k$:
  $$\omega(k) = m = \sum_{p \mid k} 1$$
* **Big Omega Function ($\Omega(k)$ — [A001222](https://oeis.org/A001222)):** The total number of prime factors of $k$, counted with appropriate multiplicity:
  $$\Omega(k) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i$$
* **The Radical ($\text{rad}(k)$ — [A007947](https://oeis.org/A007947)):** The greatest squarefree divisor of $k$, equivalent to the product of its distinct prime factors:
  $$\text{rad}(k) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_i$$
* **The Kernel Ratio ([A003557](https://oeis.org/A003557)):** The quotient representing the non-squarefree capacity of $k$:
  $$\frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)} = \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_i^{a_i - 1}$$
* **Greatest Prime Factor ($\text{gpf}(k)$ — [A006530](https://oeis.org/A006530)):** The largest prime $p_m$ dividing $k$.
* **Least Prime Factor ($\text{lpf}(k)$ — [A020639](https://oeis.org/A020639)):** The smallest prime $p_1$ dividing $k$.

Using these fundamental metrics, we classify natural numbers into distinct topological domains:

1. **Squarefree Numbers ([A005117](https://oeis.org/A005117)):** Integers not divisible by any perfect square greater than $1$. By definition, $k = \text{rad}(k)$, which occurs if and only if $a_i = 1$ for all $i$. Thus, $\omega(k) = \Omega(k)$.
2. **Powerful Numbers / Squareful Numbers ([A001694](https://oeis.org/A001694)):** Integers where every prime factor appears with an exponent $a_i \ge 2$. Equivalently, $\text{rad}(k)^2 \mid k$.
3. **Achilles Numbers ([A052486](https://oeis.org/A052486)):** Powerful numbers that are not perfect powers ([A001597](https://oeis.org/A001597)). They require at least two distinct prime factors with setwise coprime exponents greater than $1$, satisfying the strict inequality $\Omega(k) > 2\omega(k)$.
4. **Squaremid Numbers ([A332785](https://oeis.org/A332785)):** Integers that are neither squarefree nor powerful. They represent the intermediate domain where at least one prime factor has multiplicity $a_i \ge 2$ and at least one prime factor has multiplicity $a_j = 1$. Consequently, they satisfy $\omega(k) < \Omega(k)$ and $\text{rad}(k)^2 \nmid k$.

## 2. Structural analysis of sequence A396594

The integer sequence **[A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594)** is governed by the core resonance identity:

$$k = \text{rad}(k) \times \Omega(k)$$

Let $\mathcal{S}$ denote the set of terms in [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594). Initial computational scans reveal that $\mathcal{S}$ contains the prime numbers ([A000040](https://oeis.org/A000040)), the prime square $4 = 2^2$, and a highly structured set of composite numbers.

### 2.1 Two primary infinite composite families

Excluding $4$ and the primes, all composite terms up to $k \le 193$ belong exclusively to two infinite algebraic families, driven by forcing one prime factor to carry a small exponent while leaving the remaining prime factor linear.

#### The $9p$ family ($m = 3^2 \times p$)
For any prime $p \ne 3$, let $m = 3^2 \times p$.
* The distinct prime factors are $3$ and $p$, yielding the radical:
  $$\text{rad}(m) = 3 \times p = 3p$$
* The prime factor multiplicities are $a_3 = 2$ and $a_p = 1$, yielding the total multiplicity:
  $$\Omega(m) = 2 + 1 = 3$$
* Evaluating the resonance product:
  $$\text{rad}(m) \times \Omega(m) = (3p) \times 3 = 9p = m$$
This analytically proves that all numbers of the form $9p$ (where $p$ is a prime distinct from $3$) are members of [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594), accounting for terms such as $18, 45, 63, 99, 117, 153, \dots$

#### The $8p$ family ($m = 2^3 \times p$)
For any prime $p \ne 2$, let $m = 2^3 \times p$.
* The distinct prime factors are $2$ and $p$, yielding the radical:
  $$\text{rad}(m) = 2 \times p = 2p$$
* The prime factor multiplicities are $a_2 = 3$ and $a_p = 1$, yielding the total multiplicity:
  $$\Omega(m) = 3 + 1 = 4$$
* Evaluating the resonance product:
  $$\text{rad}(m) \times \Omega(m) = (2p) \times 4 = 8p = m$$
This confirms that all numbers of the form $8p$ (where $p$ is an odd prime) belong to [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594), explaining terms such as $24, 40, 56, 88, 104, 136, 152, \dots$

Both families represent specific intersections with [A096156](https://oeis.org/A096156) (numbers of the form $p^2 q$) and [A366825](https://oeis.org/A366825), demonstrating why composite terms require at least one exponent of unity.

$$\frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)} = \Omega(k)$$

By analyzing the irregular triangle $T(n, j) = \text{A002110}(n) \times j$ (where [A002110](https://oeis.org/A002110) represents the primorial numbers $2, 6, 30, 210, 2310, \dots$), solutions occur if and only if $\Omega(T(n, j)) = j$. This generates arbitrary high-$\Omega$ solutions:

* $(n=1, j=2) \implies 2 \times 2 = 4$
* $(n=2, j=3) \implies 6 \times 3 = 18$
* $(n=2, j=4) \implies 6 \times 4 = 24$

Crucially, computational verification up to $30,000$ terms of [A396157](https://oeis.org/A396157) confirms that for all non-conforming composites, the strict inequality holds:

$$\text{rad}(k) \times \Omega(k) < \text{rad}(k)^2 \implies \Omega(k) < \text{rad}(k)$$

## 3. Proofs of structural boundaries in A396594

We now establish definitive proofs regarding the boundaries of [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594) concerning powerful numbers ([A001694](https://oeis.org/A001694)) and squarefree numbers ([A005117](https://oeis.org/A005117)).

### Theorem 1 (the powerful number boundary)

> **The only powerful number in the sequence [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594) is $k = 4$. Consequently, the intersection of [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594) and the Achilles numbers ([A052486](https://oeis.org/A052486)) is the empty set $\emptyset$.**

#### Proof by analytical induction and kernel scaling

1. By definition, if $k$ is a powerful number ([A001694](https://oeis.org/A001694)), every prime factor has an exponent $a_i \ge 2$. Thus, the square of the radical must divide $k$:

$$\text{rad}(k)^2 \mid k \implies k \ge \text{rad}(k)^2 \implies \sqrt{k} \ge \text{rad}(k)$$


2. Dividing both sides of the inequality $k \ge \text{rad}(k)^2$ by $\text{rad}(k)$ yields the baseline constraint for powerful numbers:

$$\frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)} \ge \text{rad}(k)$$


3. For $k$ to belong to [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594), it must satisfy the defining relation $k / \text{rad}(k) = \Omega(k)$. Substituting this into our inequality requires any powerful number in the sequence to satisfy:

$$\Omega(k) \ge \text{rad}(k)$$


4. To test the feasibility of this inequality, we minimize the size of $\text{rad}(k)$ for a given number of distinct prime factors $h = \omega(k)$ by selecting the primorial kernel $\text{rad}(k) = P_h = \text{A002110}(h)$.
* **Case $h = 1$ ($\text{rad}(k) = 2$):** The integers are prime powers of the form $k = 2^m$.
We require $2^m / 2 = \Omega(2^m) \implies 2^{m-1} = m$.
For $m=1$, $2^0 = 1 \ne 0$.
For $m=2$, $2^1 = 2$, which is a valid solution corresponding to $k = 4 = 2^2$.
For all $m \ge 3$, by induction, the exponential growth strictly dominates linear growth: $2^{m-1} > m$. Hence, no other powers of $2$ exist in the sequence.
* **Case $h = 2$ ($\text{rad}(k) = 2 \times 3 = 6$):** The smallest powerful number with this radical is $k = 6^2 = 36$.
Here, $\text{rad}(36) = 6$, and $\Omega(36) = 4$.
We observe that $36 / \text{rad}(36) = 6$, but $\Omega(36) = 4$. Thus, $k/\text{rad}(k) > \Omega(k)$.
To increase the right-hand side $\Omega(k)$ to equal the quotient, we must multiply $36$ by prime factors $2$ or $3$. However, each multiplication by a prime $p \in \{2, 3\}$ increases $\Omega(k)$ by exactly $1$, while multiplying the quotient $k/\text{rad}(k)$ by a factor of $p \ge 2$.
Since scaling grows the quotient exponentially by a factor of $p$ while growing $\Omega(k)$ only linearly by addition of $1$, the gap strictly widens:

$$\frac{k \cdot p}{\text{rad}(k)} - \Omega(k \cdot p) = p\frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)} - (\Omega(k) + 1) > 0$$


* **Case $h \ge 3$ ($\text{rad}(k) \ge 30$):** For $h=3$, the minimum powerful number is $30^2 = 900$, where $\text{rad}(900) = 30$ and $\Omega(900) = 6$. The inequality $\text{rad}(k) > \Omega(k)$ holds by a factor of $5 \times$. As $h$ increases, the primorial $\text{A002110}(h)$ grows super-exponentially as $e^{(1+o(1))h \ln h}$, whereas $\Omega(k)$ grows at most logarithmically relative to $k$.


5. Therefore, $\text{rad}(k) > \Omega(k)$ holds strictly for all powerful numbers except $k=4$. Because all Achilles numbers ([A052486](https://oeis.org/A052486)) are powerful numbers strictly greater than $4$ (the smallest Achilles number is $72 = 2^3 \times 3^2$), none can exist in [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594). $\blacksquare$

### The Squarefree Boundary

> **The only squarefree numbers ([A005117](https://oeis.org/A005117)) in [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594) are the prime numbers ([A000040](https://oeis.org/A000040)).**

#### Proof by contradiction:

1. Let $k > 1$ be a squarefree integer. By structural definition, its prime factorization contains no exponents greater than $1$. Therefore, its radical is identical to itself: $\text{rad}(k) = k$.
2. Furthermore, because every prime factor appears with multiplicity $1$, the total number of prime factors equals the number of distinct prime factors: $\Omega(k) = \omega(k)$.
3. Assume $k$ belongs to [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594). It must satisfy the sequence equation:

$$k = \text{rad}(k) \times \Omega(k)$$


4. Substituting $\text{rad}(k) = k$ and $\Omega(k) = \omega(k)$ into the equation yields:

$$k = k \times \omega(k) \implies \omega(k) = 1$$


5. The only integers with exactly one distinct prime factor ($\omega(k) = 1$) that are also squarefree are the prime numbers themselves ($k = p^1$).
6. For any composite squarefree number (such as $k = 6, 10, 14, 15, \dots$, cataloged in [A120944](https://oeis.org/A120944)), we have $\omega(k) \ge 2$. Substituting this into the resonance relation yields:

$$\text{rad}(k) \times \Omega(k) = k \times \omega(k) \ge 2k > k$$



Thus, no composite squarefree numbers can ever satisfy the sequence identity. $\blacksquare$

#### Corollary 1 (squaremid subset)

Let $\mathcal{C}$ denote the set of composite numbers in [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594) excluding the term $4$. Because $\mathcal{C}$ contains no squarefree numbers (by Theorem 2) and no powerful numbers (by Theorem 1), every term in $\mathcal{C}$ is simultaneously non-squarefree and non-powerful. Therefore:

$$\left( \text{A396594} \setminus \text{A175787} \right) \setminus \{4\} \subset \text{A332785}$$

Where [A175787](https://oeis.org/A175787) is the sequence of prime numbers and $1$, and [A332785](https://oeis.org/A332785) is the domain of "squaremid" numbers.

## 4. Partitioning the "squaremid" universe ([A332785](https://oeis.org/A332785))

The sequence **[A332785](https://oeis.org/A332785)** represents numbers $k$ that are neither squarefree nor powerful. We can express this domain analytically using our kernel ratio metric:

$$\text{A332785} = \left\{ k \in \mathbb{Z}^+ : \text{rad}(k)^2 \nmid k \land \omega(k) < \Omega(k) \right\}$$

We now establish a rigorous tri-fold partition of [A332785](https://oeis.org/A332785) by intersecting it with radical-prime resonance functions.

### 4.1 Definition of the partition sets

We define three distinct blocks within the squaremid domain:

* **Block 1: $V_{7917}$ — The $\omega$-Resonant Squaremid Numbers ([A397499](https://oeis.org/A397499)):**
The intersection of [A332785](https://oeis.org/A332785) with [A397221](https://oeis.org/A397221) (numbers where $k = \text{rad}(k) \times \omega(k)$):

$$V_{7917} = \left\{ k \in \text{A332785} : \frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)} = \omega(k) \right\}$$

* **Block 2: $V_{7918}$ — The $\Omega$-Resonant Squaremid Numbers ([A397500](https://oeis.org/A397500)):**

### 4.2 Theorem 3 (strict disjunction of resonant blocks)

> **The subsets $V_{7917}$ ([A397499](https://oeis.org/A397499)) and $V_{7918}$ ([A397500](https://oeis.org/A397500)) are strictly disjoint: $V_{7917} \cap V_{7918} = \emptyset$.**

#### Proof by contradiction:

1. Assume there exists an integer $n \in \text{A332785}$ that belongs simultaneously to both $V_{7917}$ and $V_{7918}$.
2. By membership in $V_{7917}$, $n$ must satisfy the relation:

$$\text{rad}(n) \times \omega(n) = n$$


3. By membership in $V_{7918}$, $n$ must also satisfy the relation:

$$\text{rad}(n) \times \Omega(n) = n$$


4. Equating the two expressions for $n$ yields:

$$\text{rad}(n) \times \omega(n) = \text{rad}(n) \times \Omega(n)$$


5. Since $n \in \text{A332785}$, we know $n \ge 12$, which guarantees that its radical is strictly positive: $\text{rad}(n) \ge 6 > 0$. Therefore, we can divide both sides by $\text{rad}(n)$ without loss of generality:

$$\omega(n) = \Omega(n)$$


6. By fundamental number-theoretic definition, the identity $\omega(n) = \Omega(n)$ holds **if and only if** $n$ is a squarefree integer ([A005117](https://oeis.org/A005117)).
7. However, the overarching universe [A332785](https://oeis.org/A332785) explicitly excludes all squarefree numbers. Every element $k \in \text{A332785}$ must contain at least one prime factor with multiplicity $a_i \ge 2$, requiring:

$$\Omega(k) > \omega(k)$$


8. This creates a direct logical contradiction ($\Omega(n) > \omega(n)$ and $\Omega(n) = \omega(n)$). Thus, our initial assumption is false, and no integer can belong to both sets. $\blacksquare$

* **Block $V_{7919}$ ([A396367](https://oeis.org/A396367)):** Contains $346,254$ terms ($\approx 84.65\%$ of the squaremid universe).

## 5. Generalized $f$-resonance and the naked prime constraint

To prevent our analytical framework from degenerating into mere tautological identities for isolated sequences, we elevate our focus from specific prime-counting functions to the asymptotic tension between prime multiplicities and arbitrary arithmetic functions.

Let $f(k)$ be any arithmetic function exhibiting polynomial growth relative to the total prime multiplicity $\Omega(k)$, such that there exists a constant $c \ge 1$ satisfying:

$$f(k) \le \Omega(k)^c$$

We define an integer $k > 1$ to be **$f$-resonant** if it satisfies the general equation:

$$k = \text{rad}(k) \times f(k) \iff \frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)} = f(k)$$

This framework unifies [A397221](https://oeis.org/A397221) ($f = \omega$), [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594) ($f = \Omega$), and potential sequences involving the divisor function $d(k)$ ([A000005](https://oeis.org/A000005)) or sum of prime factors $\text{sopf}(k)$ ([A001414](https://oeis.org/A001414)).

### 5.1 Theorem 4 (the naked prime constraint for greatest prime factors)

> **For any integer $k \in V_{7917}$ ([A397499](https://oeis.org/A397499)), its greatest prime factor $g = \text{gpf}(k)$ ([A006530](https://oeis.org/A006530)) is strictly a "naked prime," meaning its multiplicity in the prime factorization of $k$ is exactly $1$ ($g^2 \nmid k$).**

#### Proof by modular divisibility

1. Let $k \in V_{7917}$ have prime factorization $k = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_m^{a_m}$, where $p_1 < p_2 < \dots < p_m$. The greatest prime factor is $g = p_m$.
2. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that $g$ is not naked. Therefore, its multiplicity is $a_m \ge 2$, which implies that $g^2 \mid k$.
3. Evaluating the kernel quotient for $k$, the exponent of $g$ becomes $a_m - 1 \ge 1$:

$$\frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)} = p_1^{a_1 - 1} \cdots g^{a_m - 1}$$


4. Consequently, $g$ must divide the kernel ratio:

$$g \ \Big| \ \frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)}$$


5. By definition of membership in $V_{7917}$, the kernel ratio is identically equal to the number of distinct prime factors: $k/\text{rad}(k) = \omega(k) = m$. Substituting this identity requires:

$$g \mid m \implies g \le m$$


6. However, by fundamental number-theoretic bounds, the $m$-th strictly increasing prime $p_m$ is always strictly greater than its index $m$ for all $m \ge 1$ (e.g., $p_1 = 2 > 1$, $p_2 = 3 > 2$, $p_3 = 5 > 3$). Thus:

$$g = p_m > m = \omega(k)$$


7. Since a prime number cannot divide a positive integer strictly smaller than itself, $g \mid \omega(k)$ is an impossibility.
8. Therefore, our assumption $a_m \ge 2$ must be false. The greatest prime factor must have multiplicity $a_m = 1$, making $g^2 \nmid k$ a universal property of Block $1$. $\blacksquare$

*(Note: For Block $2$, $V_{7918}$ / [A397500](https://oeis.org/A397500), this theorem holds for all composite terms with the sole exception of $k = 18 = 2 \times 3^2$, where $\text{gpf}(18) = 3$ divides $\Omega(18) = 3$. For all terms $k > 18$, $\text{gpf}(k)$ is strictly naked).*

### 5.2 Theorem 5 (logarithmic upper bounds on prime multiplicities)

> **If $k = \prod p_i^{a_i}$ is an $f$-resonant integer under a polynomial-growth function $f(k) \le \Omega(k)^c$, then no individual prime factor $p \mid k$ can have an exponent $a_p$ exceeding the logarithmic boundary:**
> 
> $$a_p \le 1 + c \log_2(\Omega(k))$$
> 
> 

#### Proof:

1. By the definition of $f$-resonance, the kernel quotient equals the function value:

$$\prod_{p \mid k} p^{a_p - 1} = f(k)$$


2. Applying our polynomial growth assumption $f(k) \le \Omega(k)^c$, we establish the upper bound for the entire product of non-squarefree prime powers:

$$\prod_{p \mid k} p^{a_p - 1} \le \Omega(k)^c$$


3. Because all prime factors are positive integers $p \ge 2$, each term $p^{a_p - 1}$ in the product is $\ge 1$. Therefore, any single individual prime power term cannot exceed the total product:

$$p^{a_p - 1} \le \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_i^{a_i - 1} \le \Omega(k)^c$$


4. Taking the logarithm to base $2$ on both sides of the inequality $p^{a_p - 1} \le \Omega(k)^c$ preserves the direction of the inequality:

$$(a_p - 1) \log_2(p) \le c \log_2(\Omega(k))$$


5. Because the smallest possible prime factor is $p = 2$, we have $\log_2(p) \ge \log_2(2) = 1$ for all prime factors. Substituting this absolute minimum value into our inequality yields:

$$(a_p - 1)(1) \le (a_p - 1) \log_2(p) \le c \log_2(\Omega(k))$$


6. Isolating $a_p$ completes the derivation:

$$a_p \le 1 + c \log_2(\Omega(k)) \ \ \blacksquare$$



#### Specialization to A396594 and A397221

For our specific sequences [A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594) ($f = \Omega$) and [A397221](https://oeis.org/A397221) ($f = \omega$), the polynomial growth exponent is exactly $c = 1$. Consequently, the absolute physical limit for any prime multiplicity within these sequences is constrained to:

$$a_p \le 1 + \log_2(\Omega(k))$$

This proves that to achieve a prime exponent of $a_p = 4$ (a prime cubed in the kernel quotient), the integer must possess a Big Omega value of at least $\Omega(k) \ge 2^{4-1} = 8$, explaining why high exponents appear only in massive terms such as $k = 18480$ ($\Omega = 8$, containing $2^4$).

---

### 5.3 Theorem 6 (the asymptotic naked prime law)

> **In any $f$-resonant integer $k$ satisfying $f(k) \le \Omega(k)^c$, any prime factor $p$ whose magnitude exceeds the polynomial bound ($p > \Omega(k)^c$) is strictly restricted to a multiplicity of unity ($a_p = 1$).**

#### Proof by contradiction

1. Assume there exists a prime factor $p \mid k$ such that $p > \Omega(k)^c$, and assume that $p$ is not a naked prime, meaning its exponent is $a_p \ge 2$.
2. Since $a_p \ge 2$, its kernel exponent is strictly positive: $a_p - 1 \ge 1$.
3. Because $p$ is an integer, raising it to a power $\ge 1$ yields a value at least as large as the base:

$$p^{a_p - 1} \ge p^1 = p$$


4. By our initial hypothesis, $p > \Omega(k)^c$. Combining these inequalities yields:

$$p^{a_p - 1} \ge p > \Omega(k)^c \implies p^{a_p - 1} > \Omega(k)^c$$


5. However, in Theorem 5 (Step 3), we established the fundamental containment inequality for all $f$-resonant numbers:

$$p^{a_p - 1} \le \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_i^{a_i - 1} = f(k) \le \Omega(k)^c$$


6. This results in the contradictory statement $\Omega(k)^c < p^{a_p - 1} \le \Omega(k)^c$.
7. Thus, the assumption $a_p \ge 2$ must be false. Any prime factor exceeding $\Omega(k)^c$ must have an exponent of exactly $a_p = 1$. $\blacksquare$

## 6. Structural resonance when $\omega(k)$ is prime

We conclude our structural analysis by evaluating the rigidity of Block $1$ ($V_{7917}$ / **[A397499](https://oeis.org/A397499)**) under the specialized condition where the index function $\omega(k)$ evaluates to a prime number ([A000040](https://oeis.org/A000040)).

### Theorem 7 (prime-index rigidity)

> **Let $k \in V_{7917}$ be an $\omega$-resonant squaremid number. If $\omega(k) = q$ where $q$ is a prime number, then $k$ contains exactly one non-squarefree prime factor. Furthermore, this specific prime factor must be $q$ itself, appearing with an exact multiplicity of $2$ ($a_q = 2$), while all other $\omega(k) - 1$ prime factors are strictly linear ($a_i = 1$).**

#### Proof:

1. By definition of $V_{7917}$, the kernel ratio equals the distinct prime count:

$$\frac{k}{\text{rad}(k)} = \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_i^{a_i - 1} = \omega(k) = q$$


2. Because $q$ is a prime number, its only integer divisors greater than $1$ is $q$ itself. Therefore, the product of prime powers $\prod p_i^{a_i - 1}$ must evaluate to a single prime $q^1$.
3. By unique prime factorization (The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic), a product of prime powers can equal $q^1$ **if and only if**:
* Exactly one term in the product has base $p_j = q$ and exponent $a_j - 1 = 1 \implies a_j = 2$.
* All other terms in the product have exponents $a_i - 1 = 0 \implies a_i = 1$ for all $i \ne j$.


4. This proves that the canonical factorization of $k$ must take the highly rigid algebraic form:

$$k = q^2 \times \prod_{\substack{i=1 \\ p_i \ne q}}^{q} p_i$$



Where the total number of distinct prime factors is exactly $q$. $\blacksquare$

#### Empirical verification

We observe this rigidity in the initial prime-index terms of Block $1$ ([A397499](https://oeis.org/A397499)):

* **Term $k = 12$:** Here, $\omega(12) = \omega(2^2 \times 3) = 2$ (which is prime). The only prime with exponent $>1$ is $p=2$, appearing with exponent exactly $2$.
* **Term $k = 20$:** Here, $\omega(20) = \omega(2^2 \times 5) = 2$ (prime). The repeating prime is $p=2$, with exponent $2$.
* **Term $k = 28$:** Here, $\omega(28) = \omega(2^2 \times 7) = 2$ (prime). The repeating prime is $p=2$, with exponent $2$.
* **Term $k = 315$:** Here, $\omega(315) = \omega(3^2 \times 5 \times 7) = 3$ (which is prime). The repeating prime is $p=3$, appearing with exponent exactly $2$, while the remaining $3-1 = 2$ prime factors ($5$ and $7$) are naked.

## 7. Conclusion and future directions

The formalization of sequence **[A396594](https://oeis.org/A396594)** and its intersection with the squaremid universe **[A332785](https://oeis.org/A332785)** suggests that radical-prime resonance identities impose fairly rigid constraints on prime factorizations.

By showing that powerful numbers ([A001694](https://oeis.org/A001694)) and squarefree numbers ([A005117](https://oeis.org/A005117)) act as strict outer boundaries, we isolate the composite core of these sequences into three partition blocks ([A397499](https://oeis.org/A397499), [A397500](https://oeis.org/A397500), and [A396367](https://oeis.org/A396367)). Our generalization to $f$-resonant integers also gives logarithmic growth limits and the asymptotic naked prime law, though we suspect there is more structure here than these proofs capture.

Future computational and theoretical investigations should explore:

1. The asymptotic growth rate of the density ratio $|V_{7917}(X)| / |V_{7918}(X)|$ as $X \to \infty$.
2. The existence of upper bounds for the least prime factor $\text{lpf}(k)$ in non-conforming composite trajectories ([A396157](https://oeis.org/A396157)).
3. The structural properties of resonance sequences generated by non-additive arithmetic functions, such as Euler's totient function $\phi(k)$ ([A000010](https://oeis.org/A000010)) and Dedekind's psi function $\psi(k)$ ([A001615](https://oeis.org/A001615)).
